Agriculture operates at the intersection of economic viability and environmental stewardship, with each domain profoundly influencing the other #ref. Economic incentives often drive farming practices, yet these practices can have significant environmental repercussions #ref. Conversely, environmental policies and conditions shape agricultural productivity and profitability. Understanding this interplay is crucial for developing sustainable agricultural systems that balance economic and environmental objectives #ref.

The Australian winegrowing industry exemplifies this complex relationship, contributing significantly to the national economy while facing challenges such as climate variability #ref, resource constraints #ref, and evolving market demands #ref. Sustainability in this sector requires optimizing vineyard operations such as water use, fuel consumption, and agrochemical application, not only to maintain economic viability but also to mitigate environmental impact #ref [MDPI 2024]. Studies have demonstrated that resource-intensive vineyard practices can drive short-term financial gains at the expense of long-term ecological health, underscoring the need for integrated decision-making tools #ref.

Economic drivers play a critical role in shaping sustainability outcomes in viticulture #ref. Factors such as production costs, market trends, and investment in sustainable technologies influence environmental practices #ref. For example, the shift toward premiumisation in the wine industry has encouraged sustainable vineyard management strategies that enhance wine quality while reducing environmental impact #ref. However, financial constraints—such as the high capital costs associated with renewable energy adoption and efficient irrigation systems—can hinder widespread implementation of sustainability initiatives.

In response, industry-led programs are promoting more sustainable practices #ref. In the Riverland region, the Riverland Wine Industry Blueprint prioritizes the adoption of Sustainable Winegrowing Australia accreditation #ref. Similarly, in Coonawarra, wineries such as Raidis Estate have implemented sustainability action plans focusing on minimizing resource consumption and mitigating climate change impacts #ref. These initiatives illustrate how economic and environmental factors are inherently linked, with market demands and regulatory frameworks shaping sustainable decision-making at the vineyard level #Remove?.

Scenario analysis is a valuable tool for exploring how different economic and environmental conditions impact sustainability #ref. Bayesian Networks (BNs) have been widely applied in agricultural sustainability research, as they integrate qualitative and quantitative data while accounting for uncertainty #ref [Kaikkonen et al., 2021]. Participatory modeling approaches, in which stakeholders contribute to defining system dynamics, further enhance the relevance and acceptance of model outcomes #Ref[Scrieciu et al., 2021].

Causal networks, which serve as the foundation for Bayesian Networks, provide a structured way to visualize interactions between key sustainability factors, helping stakeholders understand complex cause-effect relationships #Ref [Döll et al., 2013]. These models support scenario-based decision-making, allowing industry stakeholders to evaluate trade-offs between economic viability and environmental impact under various future conditions.

This study aims to explore the intricate relationship between economic and environmental factors in viticulture, focusing on the Coonawarra and Riverland winegrowing regions. Specifically, it seeks to answer what are the primary economic factors driving sustainability in these regions? Along with this, how do these factors interact with environmental factors? and what the implications of varying scenarios have for economic and environmental outcomes.

By integrating economic Bayesian Networks with existing environmental models, this research provides a nuanced perspective on sustainability trade-offs in viticulture. Through the application of linear pooling and scenario analysis, the study aims to generate insights that can inform both industry practices and policy development, fostering a sustainable future for these key winegrowing regions.

\subsection{Bayesian network development}
% This builds upon prior work from Boyd et al 2025
% How was the expert elicitation conducted
BNs are graphical models used for reasoning under uncertainty \cite{pearl1986fusion}. A BN is constructed as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) that represents factors as nodes and direct dependencies between them as arcs \cite{pearlbayesian}. These dependencies are quantified using conditional probability tables (CPTs), which describe the probability of each node's states given the states of its parent nodes. This structure allows Bayesian Networks to represent the joint probability distribution of a system by factoring it into simpler, marginal distributions. The software package GeNIe, created by Bayesfuse \cite{bayesfusion2024genie}, was used to create and calculate the probabilities of the BNs for this study. Where, the probability of any given variable is expressed by

$$P(x_{0}=x_{0})P(X_{1}, X_{2}, ... , X_{n}) = p(n|x_{parents}) \prod^{n}_{i=1} P(X_{i}) | Parents (X_{i})) $$,

where $X_{i}$ represents a node, and $Parents(X_{i})$ are its immediate predecessors in the network. CPTs can be populated using both data and elicitated expert knowledge, with this study using the latter to build upon the prior study by \cite{environmental_bn}.

This process involved following the format in \cite{korb2010bayesian, pourret2008bayesian}. Iteratively progresses through problem definition, structure, parameterisation, and then validation. Workshops were used in conjunction with a web app to facilitate the process. The addition of a web app followed a similar style to \cite{nyberg2022bard}. Initially, the BARD (Bayesian ARgumentation via Delphi) application was directly explored to assist in the BN construction. However, workshops being predominantly in-person led to the use of a custom web app based on the data entry capabilities of BARD to assist with quantification after workshops. This was due to participant time and distance constraints, with a further consideration being the need to customise inputs to align with the use of linear interpolation across binary classes to calculate CPT values.

% https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/risa.13772
The use of these methodologies is discussed in greater depth by \cite{werner2017expert}, where the burden of elicitation on experts is examined both in terms of the volume of assessments required and the complexity of the scenarios presented. Various elicitation approaches have been explored, including piecewise interpolation \cite{wisse2008elicit} and structured probabilistic techniques such as the noisy-OR and noisy-MAX methods \cite{diez1993parameter, pearl2014probabilistic}. A comparative study by Wisse et al. \cite{wisse2008relieving} evaluated three elicitation techniques: a likelihood-based method, a piecewise linear interpolation approach for Bayesian Belief Networks (EBBN), and a weighted sum method. To ensure continuity with prior research, the ranking and interpolation method used previously was maintained in this study.

During workshop discussions, participants found it most intuitive to express a factor's influence on its child node using a scale from 1 to 10. This ranking was then integrated with the selected elicitation method to calculate probabilities using the following formula:

\begin{equation} p = x \sum_{i}^{n} \alpha_{i} + y \sum_{i}^{m} \beta_{i} \end{equation}

where $n$ and $m$ represent the counts of factors in ideal and non-ideal states, respectively. The boundary cases, when all contributing factors are either ideal ($p_{t}$) or not ideal ($p_{f}$), are given by:

\begin{equation} p_t = x \sum_{i}^{n} \alpha_{i}, \quad p_f = y \sum_{i}^{m} \beta_{i} \end{equation}

The values of $x$ and $y$ were determined by summing the weights assigned to the Bayesian Network structure. A smooth interpolation was assumed between worst- and best-case scenarios, ensuring that intermediate states were proportionally weighted based on their relative influence. This approach allowed for a structured yet flexible method of probability assignment while minimising the cognitive burden on experts.

More types of elicitation techniques exist that have been developed to over come the challenges due to the cognitive burden on experts, particularly in complex domains (Werner and Sjökvist, 2017). Each method requiring different levels of expertise, for example, Hansson and Sjökvist (2013) compared three approaches: the likelihood method, which demands an understanding of probability distributions and log-likelihoods; the piecewise linear interpolation method (EBBN), which requires defining rows of conditional probability tables (CPTs); and the weighted sum method, which assigns relative weights to parent nodes. While the likelihood method produced the most robust results, it also placed the highest technical demands on experts.

Alternative approaches focus on reducing the number of elicited parameters while maintaining accuracy. Cain’s linear interpolation method (Cain, 2001) constructs CPTs by eliciting probabilities for “best” and “worst” scenarios and interpolating between them. Taguchi’s orthogonal arrays (Taguchi and Konishi, 1987) use a fractional factorial design to balance parent states across a reduced number of scenarios. Alkhairy and Low-Choy (2017) found that while the combined use of these two methods improved accuracy, it doubled the elicitation burden.

The suitability of these methods depends largely on the comfort of experts with different mathematical expressions of events. Where some techniques, such as ranking and interpolation, are preferable when domain experts lack formal training in probability distributions (Hassall et al., 2019). Given these considerations, this study followed the approach used in prior work, maintaining continuity with a ranking-based interpolation method to construct CPTs efficiently while minimising the cognitive load on experts but allowing for high complexity within the BN's structure.

\subsection{Linear Pooling}
% Kerrie's airport models
% https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/20/3/209
% The information, resources and time required to quantify EEBNs in itself has raised methodological questions by researchers #ref. Authors have proposed the use of many different systems in which to help this process. And furthermore, how the opinions of multiple experts can be represented together within a BN. 

The combining of experts’ opinions within BNs has received considerable attention within the Bayesian community, and many methods have been proposed. A review of these methods is presented by \cite{genest1986combining} and further discussed by \cite{ohagan2006uncertain} and more recently by \cite{albert2012combining}. As highlighted by \cite{ohagan2006uncertain}, Pooling is a prominent approach among these methods. With many different types of Pooling, such as logarithmic \cite{genest1984aggregating}, nonlinear geometric combination \cite{faria2008geometric}, and linear Pooling \cite{mcconway1981marginalization}. Further methods have recently been developed to enable nonlinear propagations \cite{volodina2024propagatingmomentsprobabilisticgraphical}, measurement error \cite{farr2020combining}, adversarial learning \cite{rios2023adversarial} and causal systems, including the definition of generic algebraic classes \cite{ramiah2024bayesian} all within the context of BNs.

A common approach among these methods is to combine probabilities, expert or otherwise, using linear pooling \cite{mcconway1981marginalization}. This method of combining knowledge from multiple experts was investigated by \cite{farr2018prior}, listing the two approaches of prior and posterior pooling. Chosen of the two for this study was Prior Linear Pooling (PrLP) due to its ability to be reviewed straight away for predictive and intercausal reasoning \cite{korb2010bayesian}, allowing experts to rapidly iterate on the structure and results of the BN even during the workshops as well as being able to incorporate elements from the prior environmental BN and the economic BN.

In the general form this approach combines probabilities $P_{i}(X)$ by calculating,
$$P(X)=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\omega_{i} P_{i}(X)$$,
where $\omega_{i}$ are positive weights given to each of the experts; and for all $n$ experts,
$$\sum^{n}_{i=1}\omega_{i}=1$$.
As with \cite{farr2018prior}, each of the experts is given equal weighting in this study.

In this study, linear pooling was employed to aggregate probabilities for nodes shared between the newly developed Economic BN and the pre-existing Environmental BN. Shared nodes were combined by pooling their probabilities, assigning equal weight to contributions from both networks. This approach ensured that the resulting model equally reflected the economic and environmental dimensions of sustainability, maintaining a balanced representation of their respective influences. By linking the BNs through shared nodes, the combined network provided a unified platform for exploring interactions between economic and environmental factors. PrLP was particularly valuable for constructing a BN that balanced economic and environmental considerations. By combining probabilities at nodes shared by both networks, the resulting model captured the interconnected influences between these two domains.

\subsection{Study regions}
% How and why were these regions chosen
Due to their distinct environmental and economic characteristics, Coonawarra and the Riverland were selected as case studies for this research. These regions, previously studied in the context of environmental sustainability, provide an ideal foundation for expanding the analysis to include economic sustainability considerations.

Located in South Australia's Limestone Coast zone, Coonawarra is distinguished by its cooler climate and unique terra rossa soils, which contribute to its reputation for producing high-quality wines, particularly Cabernet Sauvignon~(\cite{hallidayAustralianWineEncyclopedia2009}). The region's focus on premium wine production necessitates vineyard practices that balance resource efficiency with maintaining high grape quality. However, economic factors such as production costs, market positioning, and consumer demand for premium wines also play a critical role in shaping sustainability strategies.

In contrast, the Riverland is an inland region characterized by a continental climate, lower disease pressure, and a strong reliance on irrigation from the Murray River. The Riverland is a high-production region growing Cabernet Sauvignon, Shiraz, and Chardonnay, with an industry structure geared toward large-scale, cost-efficient viticulture~(\cite{hallidayAustralianWineEncyclopedia2009}). The economic drivers in this region are distinct from those in Coonawarra, with a focus on production efficiency, water management, and maintaining profitability in a competitive global market.

Integration of Economic and Environmental Factors
Building on prior research, this study extends the Bayesian Network (BN) models developed for environmental sustainability by integrating economic factors into the framework. The expert advisory panel recommended refining the models for Coonawarra and Riverland to capture both environmental and economic trade-offs. This involved incorporating factors such as operational costs, market-driven incentives, and capital investments in sustainable technologies, alongside existing environmental variables like water use, soil management, and fuel consumption.

As in the previous study, region-specific BNs were developed based on the structure of the original environmental BN. Site-specific conditional probabilities were incorporated and scrutinized by the expert panel to reflect differences in how economic and environmental factors interact. In Coonawarra, the models accounted for the economic impacts of maintaining premium wine quality, including the cost of labor-intensive vineyard management and investments in sustainability initiatives. In Riverland, the focus was on the financial viability of high-volume production and the role of irrigation costs and water pricing in sustainability decisions.

Workshops were conducted to refine the models, with discussions emphasizing the role of economic pressures in sustainability decision-making. In Coonawarra, expert input resulted in two distinct BN models, reflecting differing vineyard management approaches—one prioritizing opportunity-based tractor use for under-vine operations, while the other emphasized more structured under-vine management and soil conservation strategies. In Riverland, the expert panel reached a greater consensus, leading to a single BN model that reflects the region’s emphasis on cost-effective vineyard practices and water-efficient strategies.

By incorporating economic considerations into these region-specific BN models, this study aims to provide a more holistic understanding of sustainability in Australian viticulture, offering insights that can inform both industry practices and policy interventions.

\subsection{Definitions}

The critical economic factors identified by the industry panel and their agreed definitions are presented in Table~\ref{tab:definitions}. To ensure consistency in analysis, each variable was classified into a binary state: an "ideal" scenario (economically favourable) or a "non-ideal" scenario (economically unfavourable). For example, Fuel Use was categorized as low (ideal) or high (non-ideal) based on operational efficiency and cost per litre. Similarly, Water Use was assessed as efficient (ideal) or inefficient (non-ideal), considering both the total volume used and the cost per megaliter. Labour Costs, Repair and Maintenance Expenses, and Electricity Costs were similarly categorized based on industry benchmarks, reflecting financial sustainability in vineyard operations.

Other factors, such as Grape Quality and Yield Targets, Winery Contract Certainty, and Market Supply and Demand, were also assigned binary classifications to capture their economic impact on vineyard profitability. Additionally, variables related to equipment and operational efficiency—such as Equipment Age, Equipment Use in Hours, and Specialist Equipment Needs—were classified to reflect their role in cost management and long-term investment strategies.

By applying this binary classification system, the study translates complex economic considerations into a measurable framework, allowing for direct comparisons across factors. This approach was essential for constructing the Economic Bayesian Network (BN), ensuring that key economic drivers and trade-offs were accurately captured.

\subsection{Economic Bayesian Networks}

The Economic BNs for Coonawarra and the Riverland contained structural differences reflecting the distinct regional relationships of their economic factors. Aligned with prior work, with structural differences highlighting the distinctions in the operational utility of 'Tractor Passes' through Coonawarra connecting 'Fertiliser Use' to 'Tractor Passes' and the Riverland not. Similarly, Coonawarra connected 'Vineyard Floor Management' to 'Contract Labor', reflecting the region’s use of external contractors for these tasks. The differences in operations were discussed during workshops and found to be predominantly due to the changing priorities of different vineyards and regions. Furthermore, the discussion highlighted the existence of contextual information that was difficult to incorporate due to its temporal nature, which was key to the why behind these reasons, with examples including planning around contractor availability due to relying on their time schedules, the need for responses to time-sensitive issues such as maintenance and breakages, and unforeseeable logistical factors. A further difference was the link between 'Water Scarcity' and 'Water Use' in the Riverland model, reflecting the region’s resource-sensitive environment. Unlike Coonawarra, where water licences are more widely available, Riverland growers are more subject to seasonal scarcity, influencing both costs and vineyard productivity.

Notably, both economic BNs have three points of origin, that is, three critical influences that are parent nodes to all of the factors influencing 'Economic Impact'. These are water resource management, 'Seasonal Conditions' and 'Market Supply and Demand'. Noting that water resource management is driven by the three factors 'Water Use Efficiency', 'Water Licences' and 'Water Quality', with these nodes only directly influencing water and nothing else; the outcome of 'Water Use' is further driven by other factors and somewhat within the control of vineyard managers. Of the BN's three critical drivers, 'Economic Impact' is directly connected to 'Market Supply and Demand', due to 'Grape Revenue' being connected only by 'Yield' as opposed to 'Operating Cost' having a path of several nodes. This level of connectivity is closely followed by 'Seasonal Conditions', which is equidistant from 'Grape Revenue' and 'Operating Cost'. An interesting point regarding these connections was made during workshops as to how vineyard operations tend to adapt to these outside factors, changing yield targets and thus input resources and whether a vineyard would even choose to operate over a season depending upon projected climates and market prices.

\subsection{Pooled Bayesian Networks}

Combining the economic BNs with the previously developed environmental BNs (\cite{boyd2025bn}, environmental BNs are also included in the appendix) resulted in a complex system with two terminal nodes. Although complex in their structure, there are several similarities between the two pooled BNs, both to their origin BNs and to each other. By introducing Environmental elements, a new set of originating factors, parents to terminal nodes, is introduced through the inclusion of the concept of soil management. Soil management, although not present in the economic BN, has influence over many of the factors it brings, such as 'Water use' and, indirectly 'Tractor Passes' and 'Fuel use'. As origin nodes, soil management is represented through 'Nutrient Leaching Management' and 'Soil Organic Carbon Management', with 'Soil Degradation Management' also being a factor but included as a child node. 

Along with the introduction of greater complexity through an increased number of factors, CPTs have been altered to include both the economic and environmental impacts. Some nodes have been influenced more than others through the pooling of worst and best-case scenarios. As a result, several nodes have become equally and oppositely weighted between their contributions to the environment and economy, creating points at which decisions equally influence the impacts of one positively as they do to the other negatively. This is a result of experts rating worst and best-case scenarios as equal or closely equal to their inverse probability ($1-p$), as well as further complexity being introduced through greater connections that operate to dilute any one parent node's contribution. However, many of these decisions, or node connections and influences, are driven by decisions not directly related in origin but have equal importance within the BN. For example, the introduction of soil management as a group of factors does not directly relate to market considerations such as 'Grape Quality and Yield Targets' but is indirectly connected through the considerations of 'Tractor Passes' and 'Fuel use'. This makes these nodes that have equal weightings between environmental impact and economy pivotal points of interaction between these external factors, points within the BNs that highlight decisions sensitive to both the economic and environmental outcomes of vineyards.

\subsection{Scenario Models}

The following scenarios assess the effects of increasing climate extremes on key economic and environmental factors in Coonawarra and the Riverland, based on insights from Wine Australia's climate research, which projects a rising likelihood of extreme weather events. To establish a baseline for comparison, Scenario 0 represents the default state of the BNs with no evidence introduced. In Scenario 1, we introduce a ‘not ideal’ climate condition within the pooled networks, reflecting a worsening climate outlook. To further emphasise the impact of extreme conditions, Scenario 2 sets all immediate child nodes of climate to ‘not ideal’, simulating a broader systemic response to adverse climate effects. These child nodes include 'Pest and Disease Management', 'Spray Application Number', 'Adverse Events' and 'Water Quality'. Note that between the two BNs, the structure differs, resulting in 'Water Scarcity' also being a child node of 'Seasonal Conditions' in Coonawarra but not in the Riverland due to differing primary water sources.

Building on this, Scenario 3 incorporates tipping-point factors—nodes that, as discussed previously, are factors that negatively impact the environmental outlook while benefiting economic outcomes - by setting these nodes to ‘not ideal’ within the BN. Noting again that the structure between the two regions differs slightly, changing the selected nodes provided evidence in the final two scenarios (further described in the sections below). In contrast, Scenario 4 assigns these same nodes ‘ideal’ evidence, allowing for a direct comparison of their influence under different assumptions. Importantly, Scenarios 3 and 4 retain all other nodes in the same state as Scenario 2, ensuring that the climate outlook remains unchanged while isolating the effects of these key regional decision factors.

This final workshop integrated prior discussions on the environmental and economic Bayesian Networks, synthesizing expert-driven weightings to refine causal relationships between key viticultural factors. The outcomes of this workshop provide insights into the applicability of the model, its regional adaptability, and key sustainability considerations for viticulture.

Integration of Environmental and Economic Impacts
The Bayesian Network developed throughout this research effectively quantifies trade-offs between economic and environmental sustainability. A central feature of the model is its capacity to illustrate how resource inputs, such as water use, impact both economic viability and environmental sustainability. The expert-driven weightings incorporated during the workshops streamlined the calibration of the model, reducing the need for further manual adjustments and allowing for an integrated assessment of sustainability trade-offs.

Regional Focus and Model Granularity
The model was designed to capture broad regional trends, though finer-scale factors, such as soil heterogeneity within subregions, were found to have limited influence at this level. Climate change emerged as a significant driver of practice change, particularly in relation to vineyard management decisions such as spray application timing and input intensity. Experts noted that increasing climatic variability has led to greater reliance on chemical interventions while simultaneously introducing constraints on their application due to shifting weather patterns.

Model Adaptability and Practical Applications
The Bayesian Network was recognized for its adaptability, allowing users to test changes in prioritization and assess the feasibility of different viticultural strategies. The interactive nature of the model enables scenario testing, where users can explore potential outcomes based on different input configurations. Future applications could further enhance this adaptability by allowing growers to input specific vineyard data. Where empirical data is unavailable, expert knowledge can be leveraged to inform model predictions, thereby bridging data gaps in decision-making.

Key Economic and Environmental Considerations
Several critical economic and environmental factors were emphasized during the workshop:

Water Use and Irrigation Efficiency: Experts identified infrastructure maintenance, real-time monitoring, and targeted incentives for efficiency improvements as crucial elements in managing water resources sustainably. While economic considerations often drive water use decisions, ensuring irrigation efficiency was recognized as a key factor in reducing environmental impact.
Capital Investment in Sustainable Practices: The role of capital investment, particularly in fuel-efficient machinery and irrigation infrastructure, was widely discussed. Experts debated the feasibility of such investments, noting that while they may contribute to long-term economic and environmental sustainability, the initial costs often present barriers to widespread adoption.
Labor and Contracting Constraints: The availability of contract labor remains a key challenge in vineyard operations. Experts highlighted the difficulties associated with contractual obligations, particularly in securing labor at optimal times for vineyard management practices such as pruning.
Challenges in Decision-Making
A key theme that emerged from the workshop was the difficulty in balancing economic and environmental objectives. Experts noted that regional wine pricing structures often do not reflect actual production quality, making it difficult for growers to achieve premiums for sustainable practices. This disconnect between production quality and market valuation creates uncertainty for growers seeking to invest in sustainable initiatives. Additionally, trade-offs between quality and yield remain a persistent challenge, requiring targeted management strategies to optimize production outcomes.

Potential Future Developments
The findings from this workshop suggest several avenues for future model refinement:

Expanded Model Scope: The incorporation of additional variables such as feral animal control, irrigation monitoring improvements, and late pruning as a climate adaptation strategy could enhance the model’s predictive capability.
Infrastructure Investment and Maintenance: Further refinement of the model could explore how maintenance costs and infrastructure investments influence long-term sustainability outcomes.
Grower-Specific Decision Support: The model's accessibility for individual growers could be improved by developing an interface that allows for real-time input adjustments, enabling them to test the implications of different management decisions under varying economic and environmental conditions.
This final expert workshop reinforced the utility of Bayesian Networks in integrating expert insights with empirical data, demonstrating their potential as dynamic tools for assessing trade-offs between economic and environmental sustainability in viticulture. The adaptability of the model suggests that it could serve as a decision-support tool for growers, policymakers, and industry stakeholders seeking to optimize vineyard management strategies under changing climatic and economic conditions.

One grower from Coonawarra noted that reducing tractor passes or shifting to minimal tillage could improve cost efficiency but could also lead to increased weed and pest pressure, which then requires more chemical intervention.
In the Riverland, the panel noted that water price volatility and allocation uncertainty are forcing growers to prioritize economic decisions over long-term environmental considerations, such as soil health and biodiversity maintenance.

\section{Discussion}

% limitations of the method:This study has demonstrated that the sustainability of viticulture in Coonawarra and Riverland is shaped by economic stability, market structures, and environmental constraints. By integrating economic and environmental Bayesian Networks (BNs), this research provides a structured framework for quantifying trade-offs, offering a nuanced perspective on how economic decisions influence environmental outcomes.

The findings suggest that economic constraints frequently override environmental considerations, with contract security, operational costs, and capital investment requirements playing dominant roles in sustainability adoption. The final expert workshop reinforced these insights, revealing the importance of market structures, financial incentives, and policy interventions in determining whether sustainability initiatives are successfully integrated into vineyard management strategies.

A core strength of this study is its equal weighting of economic and environmental Bayesian Networks, ensuring that both perspectives were proportionally integrated into decision-making scenarios. However, a notable limitation is that economic and environmental sustainability operate on different scales and involve distinct metrics that are not always directly comparable. This inherent abstraction introduces a risk of oversimplifying complex interactions and trade-offs, potentially leading to conclusions that do not fully capture the nuanced priorities or long-term implications of either domain. The challenge of integrating multiple sustainability dimensions into a unified decision-making model has been widely discussed in sustainability research. Buys et al. (2014) highlight this issue in the context of Australian dairy farming, where a triple-bottom-line approach was required to balance economic, environmental, and social sustainability objectives, yet aligning these factors within a structured framework remained difficult. Similar complexities exist in viticulture, where financial viability, regulatory requirements, and ecological stewardship intersect in ways that are often difficult to quantify within a single model.

\subsection{Scenario Interpretation}
A central finding of this study is that economic stability can be maintained under worsening climate conditions, but often at the expense of environmental outcomes. The ability of growers to buffer financial risks through cost adjustments and operational flexibility aligns with existing literature that suggests short-term economic imperatives frequently dictate vineyard management decisions, leading to increased resource use and chemical interventions (Santiago-Brown et al., 2015).

While climate variability was acknowledged as a growing constraint on vineyard operations, the study findings indicate that economic flexibility allows growers to mitigate financial risks without necessarily addressing long-term sustainability concerns. This raises important questions about the effectiveness of voluntary sustainability initiatives, particularly when the financial pressures of maintaining profitability take precedence over environmental responsibility.

The challenge of defining and quantifying sustainability in viticulture is further emphasized by Santiago-Brown et al. (2015), who identify environmental, economic, and social dimensions as core components of sustainability, each requiring distinct metrics that are often difficult to align. Similarly, the MESMIS framework, which categorizes sustainability into descriptors, indicators, and parameters, struggles to account for regional variability and the interconnectedness of economic and environmental impacts (Montalvo-Falcón et al., 2023). The subjectivity of sustainability definitions further complicates measurement, as stakeholder priorities often shape the way sustainability is assessed and implemented (Baiano, 2021). These challenges reinforce the need for adaptive and context-specific assessment frameworks, particularly in industries such as viticulture, where sustainability priorities vary between production models and regional market conditions.

Regional Sensitivities and Structural Differences
The regional comparison between Coonawarra and Riverland highlights distinct economic-environmental trade-offs, emphasizing that the structure of the wine market, rather than solely environmental conditions, dictates the extent to which sustainability considerations are integrated into vineyard management.

In Riverland, economic outcomes are more directly influenced by market fluctuations and contract security, reinforcing previous studies that highlight the risk exposure of high-volume production regions (ACCC, 2020). Growers in this region rely heavily on long-term purchasing agreements with bulk wine producers, making financial stability more dependent on contract terms than on environmental conditions. As a result, sustainability efforts in Riverland are largely driven by regulatory compliance rather than proactive environmental management.

In contrast, Coonawarra’s sustainability strategies appear more closely tied to soil conservation and water-use efficiency, reflecting the long-term priorities of premium-focused wine production. These findings are consistent with studies on premium viticulture, which suggest that higher-value market positioning allows for a greater integration of environmental sustainability into economic strategies (Baiano, 2021).

Müller et al. (2024) provide a similar perspective on participatory adaptation processes, arguing that implementation probability BNs allow stakeholders to better understand system trade-offs, thus facilitating acceptance of adaptation measures. Their findings align with this study’s conclusion that regional adaptability must be considered in sustainability decision-making, particularly in sectors where financial and environmental incentives diverge.

\subsection{Decision Points and Management Trade-offs}
This study identifies swing nodes (e.g., Tractor Passes, Water Use, and Fuel Use) as key decision points where growers must navigate economic-environmental trade-offs. Water use, in particular, emerged as a factor where economic imperatives frequently overshadow sustainability objectives. The findings suggest that resource allocation decisions are driven more by cost management than by environmental considerations, aligning with prior research that shows growers are more responsive to financial incentives and regulatory mandates than to voluntary conservation measures (Wine Australia, 2023).

The study also emphasizes the barriers to adopting capital-intensive sustainability initiatives, such as fuel-efficient machinery, soil monitoring technology, and advanced irrigation systems. While these investments offer long-term cost savings and environmental benefits, expert insights highlighted that upfront costs remain prohibitive, particularly for smaller growers. This reinforces the need for targeted financial mechanisms to bridge the gap between economic constraints and sustainability adoption.

Additionally, labor availability and contract structures were identified as key constraints on sustainable vineyard management. The difficulty in securing contract labor at optimal times was noted as a major operational challenge, affecting both cost efficiency and production quality. Growers facing labor shortages often compensate through increased mechanization or chemical inputs, further exacerbating economic-environmental trade-offs.

Another concern raised in the expert workshop was the disconnect between production quality and price premiums, which creates uncertainty for growers investing in sustainability measures. If regional pricing structures do not reflect the added value of sustainable viticulture, growers are less likely to adopt environmental best practices. This further highlights the importance of reforming contract structures and pricing mechanisms to better align market incentives with sustainability goals.


\section{Conclusion}
This study provides a probabilistic framework for evaluating economic and environmental trade-offs in Australian viticulture, demonstrating that economic risk mitigation frequently occurs at the expense of environmental outcomes. The findings reinforce that contract security, capital investment barriers, and resource pricing structures shape the feasibility of sustainability adoption more than climate awareness alone.

Müller et al. (2024) provide further evidence that participatory BNs improve the implementation probability of sustainability initiatives by aligning stakeholder expectations with adaptive decision-making strategies. Their work reinforces the conclusion that economic-environmental trade-offs are best addressed through scenario-based participatory modeling, ensuring that sustainability efforts remain viable under diverse financial and climatic conditions.

By integrating Bayesian Networks with expert insights, this study offers a structured decision-support tool for growers, policymakers, and industry stakeholders. Future research should focus on quantifying financial returns on sustainability investments, improving contract transparency, and developing policy frameworks that incentivize resilient viticulture practices. These efforts will be critical for aligning economic incentives with environmental best practices, ensuring the long-term sustainability of Australia’s wine industry.
